About

I study the exchange of mathematics and metaphors between physics and social theory in the twentieth century. My dissertation constructs a history of entropy in American science, art, and economics from the technocracy movement in the 1930s to the rise of complexity science in the 1990s. It examines how, amid the coupled economic and environmental crises of the twentieth century, social scientists and international policymakers came to adopt a specific definition of order from many-body physics as a model to understand and engineer order in society.

My research has been supported by the American Institute of Physics, the Center for the History of Political Economy at Duke, the Max Planck Institute for the History of Science, and the History & Political Economy Project at Johns Hopkins. Some of this work has been published or is forthcoming in Critical Inquiry, Historical Studies in the Natural Sciences, and Physics Today.

Before arriving at Yale, I worked as a researcher in condensed matter physics. Back then, I studied non-equilibrium electronic states, with the goal of understanding the interplay between magnetism and topology in candidate materials for high-efficiency electronics.
